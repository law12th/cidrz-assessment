CIDRZ technical assessment · Section B vertical slice

CareLink Follow-Up

Helping clinic staff find the patients who have missed an appointment — on a clinic PC, over a link that is often not there.

The problem, as I understood it

  • One list, per facility, of patients whose most recent visit set an appointment they did not keep.
  • It has to be right at 10,000 rows and on a six-year-old PC.
  • It has to keep working while the network does not.
  • It is personal health information, protected by law.

Where the brief was open, and what I assumed

  • "More than n days" is strict: exactly 7 days is not yet listed.
  • Only the most recent visit counts. Any later visit takes the patient off the list.
  • "Today" is the facility's local date, not the server's.
  • Facilities have a local server. The assumption I am least sure of — slide 4.
All data invented. No real patient or organisational data.1 / 8
Architecture

A clinic keeps working when the link does not

The path, end to end: a clinician records a visit → the row and an outbox entry are written in one local transaction, and the list updates at once → when the link is up the agent pushes the batch → national applies it idempotently and acknowledges → the district officer sees it. Offline, only the last step waits.

Same artifact, two profiles. The node is the cache that survives the outage.2 / 8
Decisions · most confident

Three I would defend

1 · A server in every clinic, not an offline-first browser app

Rejected: IndexedDB in the browser. It puts patient data on shared, old PCs under tight storage quotas, and every workstation would sync 10,000 patients alone — so two PCs in one clinic drift apart from each other. The cost I accept: hardware, and a 1,500-node fleet to operate.

2 · The whole rule runs in SQL, never in application memory

Rejected: load the facility's patients and filter in code — which is what the extract in Section C does. One LATERAL probe per patient gives ~130 ms for any page over 100,000 patients and 400,000 visits, measured, including the total count.

3 · Replay safety lives in the database, not in the cache

Rejected: Redis as the source of truth for idempotency. A unique constraint on the key holds across instances, restarts and a flushed cache. Redis only makes a replay cheap, fails open, and is written after the row is durable — so a failed insert can never suppress a real retry.

Each trade-off names what it costs, not only what it buys.3 / 8
Decisions · least confident

The facility node fleet

It is the right technical answer for outages that last days. It rests on two things I assumed rather than confirmed: that the Ministry can fund 1,500 small servers, and that districts can support them.

What would change my mind

  • The Ministry cannot fund or support the hardware.
  • Pilot data shows the 400 well-connected sites rarely actually lose their link.

What I would do instead

  • Move those well-connected sites to direct online use of national.
  • Route their lab traffic through national.
  • Keep nodes only where connectivity genuinely demands them.

Why I am telling you this rather than defending it: the decision is reversible in one direction only. Starting with nodes and removing them later is cheap; starting online and discovering the 300 offline sites cannot work is not.

The assumption, not the architecture, is the risk.4 / 8

Section D · for the whole panel

Working offline, and the duplicate problem

Why duplicates happened

The clinic queued its work while the link was down. When it came back the queue was sent, timed out halfway, and was sent again from the beginning — so the first half arrived twice. The system had no way to recognise work it had already done.

The fix, in one sentence

Every action carries its own reference number, created once in the clinic. The server records that reference in the same breath as the work itself, so the second arrival is recognised and ignored — like a cheque number, not a photocopy.

The duplicates already there

Nothing is deleted. Duplicate visits are found by rule and marked superseded, staying readable.

Duplicate patients are different. Merging two patient records merges two clinical histories, and getting it wrong is a safety event. The system only proposes a match; someone at the clinic confirms, because they can ring the patient; the decision is recorded with a name against it, and it can be undone.

Nothing merges automatically. Ever.

When Monday's clinic edit meets Tuesday's district edit, a person decides — never last-write-wins.7 / 8

Everything in Section D is designed and nothing of it is built. It is the largest gap between this submission and a system a Ministry could deploy.
